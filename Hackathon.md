***I am participating in a student hackathon and need to design and build a functional prototype for the challenge “AI-Powered Product Intelligence for Industrial Commerce.” The challenge states that industrial companies manage large amounts of product
information across multiple sources such as websites, catalogs and technical documents, and that converting this scattered information into accurate,
structured product data is difficult and time-consuming.
The objective is to build an AI-powered solution that transforms limited product information into rich, reliable and commerce-ready product intelligence, with emphasis on data enrichment, validation and explainable outputs. 
The expected outcomes are structured data generation, improved accuracy and consistency, AI-based validation and enrichment, and a scalable catalog engine. 
Treat this as a serious hackathon prototype where the judges will mainly evaluate the problem-solving approach, accuracy, consistency, scalability, performance, 
explainability and practical usefulness rather than simply the number of features or visual appearance.


****The solution must be genuinely differentiated because many student teams will ask AI tools for project ideas and will likely build generic systems 
such as PDF-to-text extraction, OCR-to-JSON, LLM-generated product descriptions, product chatbots or simple recommendation systems. 
Do not propose a generic “upload document → OCR → LLM → JSON → dashboard” solution. 
The core innovation should be an evidence-driven industrial product intelligence engine that does not merely extract information 
but determines whether product information can be trusted, enriches incomplete records, detects conflicts, validates specifications, maps 
products to real industry standards, explains every important decision and can identify technically suitable replacement products. 
The uniqueness must come from the approach and intelligence of the system rather than simply adding many unrelated features. 
Before implementation, analyze what common hackathon solutions are likely to look like, identify their weaknesses, 
explain how this solution is different, define its unique value proposition and identify the strongest two or three demo moments.

****The system must support generic industrial products rather than being designed only for motors, bearings, pumps or any other single category. 
It should be capable of handling mechanical components, electrical and electronic components, industrial automation products, motors, pumps, bearings,
valves, sensors, cables, switchgear, connectors, fasteners, tools, safety equipment, hydraulic and pneumatic components, control equipment, industrial 
machinery, spare parts and other industrial products. Motors, bearings or pumps may be used as examples in the prototype, but the architecture must be 
category-independent. Use a dynamic product attribute model in which the system identifies the product category and dynamically determines 
relevant attributes and validation rules. Do not hardcode the entire application around one product type.

*****The main system workflow should be designed as scattered or limited product information entering through an image, nameplate, PDF, technical document, catalog, 
website, CSV or manual input, followed by information extraction, product identification, attribute discovery, normalization, enrichment, 
multi-source evidence retrieval, conflict detection, engineering and data validation, industry-standard matching, confidence calculation, explainable 
decision-making, human approval and finally generation of commerce-ready structured product intelligence.
The application should also support catalog-level processing rather than only analyzing one product.

The prototype should include six major differentiating capabilities. First, Standard Parts Matching should map products to real industrial standards such as eCl@ss, UNSPSC and ETIM using verified reference data rather than allowing an LLM to invent classification codes. If an exact classification cannot be verified, clearly state that the classification is unavailable instead of hallucinating a code, and explain why a classification was selected. Second, Scan & Extract should accept machine nameplates, product labels, technical specification sheets and PDFs and extract manufacturer, product name, model, part number, specifications, units, ratings, dimensions and other relevant attributes. Image/OCR extraction should only be the input layer; the main innovation must occur after extraction. Third, an Engineering/Common-Sense Validation Engine should detect logically or technically inconsistent values, invalid formats, unit inconsistencies, mathematically suspicious relationships and product-specific conflicts. Deterministic Python or equivalent programmatic rules should be used for calculations wherever possible rather than asking an LLM to perform calculations. Results should distinguish valid information, potential warnings and conflicts, and the system should not claim certainty where engineering assumptions are required. Fourth, Trusted Source Scoring should track the source of every important attribute and determine source reliability based on source type, authority, attribute type, evidence quality, agreement with other sources and, where relevant, recency. A manufacturer datasheet may be more authoritative for technical specifications while a supplier may be more relevant for price. Confidence scores must be calculated from actual evidence and agreement rather than randomly generated. Fifth, Before & After Intelligence should clearly show the original value, AI/enriched value, reason for the change, supporting evidence and confidence, with Accept, Reject and View Evidence actions so that important AI changes require human approval. Sixth, Find a Replacement Part should identify alternatives for discontinued, unavailable or obsolete products using technical compatibility rather than simple text similarity. It should compare relevant attributes dynamically, provide a compatibility score, show matched and mismatched attributes, identify critical differences and explain why the replacement is recommended.

Additional useful intelligence can include missing-attribute prioritization, where the system identifies not only which attributes are missing but which missing attributes are critical, high, medium or low priority; conflict resolution, where contradictory values from different sources are shown rather than silently overwritten; an evidence chain showing how each final value was selected; and a Product Intelligence Passport containing product identity, technical specifications, normalized values, classification, source evidence, confidence, validation results, conflicts, missing information, replacement options and human approval status. These should strengthen the core solution rather than become unnecessary features.

Accuracy must be treated as a primary engineering requirement. Do not claim arbitrary accuracy percentages. Design an actual evaluation methodology comparing system output with ground truth and measure appropriate metrics such as attribute extraction accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, classification accuracy, normalization accuracy, conflict-detection accuracy, replacement-ranking accuracy and OCR accuracy where applicable. Also consider false positives, false negatives, unsupported AI claims and hallucinated attributes. The system should prefer “insufficient evidence” over inventing a product specification. Confidence scores must be evidence-based and explainable.

Scalability and performance are also primary requirements. Design the architecture so that the same approach can eventually process tens, hundreds, thousands or hundreds of thousands of products. Do not send every operation to an LLM. Use preprocessing, retrieval, caching, batch processing, database indexing, incremental updates and selective AI calls where appropriate. Consider and explain processing time per product, number of LLM calls, OCR time, model/API latency, database performance and batch throughput. The system should demonstrate that the approach can evolve from a prototype catalog to a large industrial product catalog without redesigning the entire system.

There is no mandatory technology stack. Do not assume that Python, Streamlit, SQLite or any specific framework must be used. Select technologies based on accuracy, performance, scalability, cost, development time, hackathon reliability and student-level feasibility. You may consider Python, Java, JavaScript/Node.js, React, FastAPI, Flask, PostgreSQL, MySQL, MongoDB, vector databases, embedding models, OCR systems, multimodal models, LLMs and cloud services if they are technically justified. However, do not over-engineer the solution with unnecessary enterprise technologies such as Kubernetes, Kafka, complex microservices, GraphQL, service meshes or complicated DevOps infrastructure. Every technology should have a clear purpose and the final stack should be understandable and achievable by engineering students. Compare reasonable technology options before selecting the final stack.

My preferred LLM API is Groq. Use Groq when an external LLM is required, keep API keys in environment variables and never hardcode credentials. Use the LLM primarily for information extraction, semantic interpretation, product categorization, attribute understanding, conflict reasoning and explanations. Use deterministic/programmatic methods for unit conversion, calculations, validation rules, confidence aggregation, database operations, filtering and compatibility calculations wherever possible. If embeddings or local models can improve scalability and reduce API calls, evaluate them as an option. Provide a fallback or mock mode if the Groq API is unavailable so the hackathon demonstration does not completely fail because of an API issue.

Do not use generic e-commerce datasets such as Amazon, Flipkart, Walmart, fashion, consumer shopping, retail transaction or product-review datasets. The data must represent industrial products. Research suitable industrial datasets from sources such as Hugging Face, Kaggle, GitHub, Zenodo, Mendeley Data, academic repositories, official standards sources and publicly available manufacturer technical documentation. For every dataset considered, verify and clearly state its exact name, platform, original publisher, source URL, license or access terms, number of products, categories, available fields, images or documents, technical attributes, suitability and limitations. If a dataset is used, the final documentation must explicitly state where it came from, who published it, its platform, URL, license and exactly what part of the project uses it. Never simply say “industrial dataset” without giving the source.

Investigate industrial-product datasets such as IndustryBench-MIPU and other relevant public datasets. IndustryBench-MIPU may be useful for evaluating industrial product image and attribute extraction, but do not make the entire project simply an image-to-JSON system because the challenge is broader. Investigate whether the dataset's current access and license conditions are appropriate before using it. If no single public dataset contains product information, technical attributes, images, source evidence, standards and replacement information, use a hybrid strategy consisting of appropriate public industrial datasets, public reference/standards data, legally usable public technical documentation, a small curated prototype dataset and clearly labelled synthetic scenarios where necessary. Clearly distinguish real data, curated data and synthetic data. Synthetic data may be used to simulate missing fields, conflicting sources or discontinued-product scenarios for demonstrating the intelligence, but never present synthetic information as real manufacturer information.

Investigate reliable sources for eCl@ss, UNSPSC and ETIM and verify whether their classification data can legally and practically be used in a student prototype. Never fabricate classification codes. If an official or verified reference cannot be used, show that the classification is unavailable rather than hallucinating it.

The system should support a generic scalable catalog engine with bulk upload, batch processing, search, filtering, sorting, product comparison, reprocessing, confidence filtering, validation-status filtering and incremental updates. It should maintain the distinction between extracted information, enriched information, inferred information, validated information and user-approved information. Important AI changes should never silently overwrite existing data. Maintain an audit trail containing original value, AI-suggested value and final approved value.

Explainability is critical. Every important AI-generated or enriched attribute should have a value, source, evidence, confidence, validation status and reason. For example, if the system changes voltage from 400 V to 415 V, it should be able to explain that the manufacturer datasheet and product nameplate indicate 415 V while a lower-trust distributor source indicates 400 V, so 415 V was selected. Explanations must be grounded in stored evidence and must not be generic LLM-generated reasoning unsupported by the actual data.

Design a professional end-to-end application navigation that can include Dashboard/Home, Product Analyzer, Data Extraction, Product Enrichment, Validation Center, Evidence & Sources, Standards Matching, Review Center, Replacement Finder, Product Catalog, Product Comparison, Product Intelligence Passport, Reports and About/Settings. Improve the navigation if a better workflow exists, but keep the main user journey simple: Add Product → Extract → Identify → Discover Attributes → Normalize → Enrich → Retrieve Evidence → Detect Conflicts → Validate → Match Standards → Calculate Confidence → Explain → Human Review → Find Replacement → Generate Final Product Record → Add to Catalog → Export. The UI should be modern, professional, clean and engineering-focused, with clear product-data quality indicators, confidence scores, evidence panels, validation status, comparison views and review controls. UI quality is important, but the technical intelligence, accuracy and approach are more important than visual decoration.

The final product should be capable of generating a structured Product Intelligence Passport and exporting the approved product information in formats such as JSON and CSV, with PDF export if useful. The final output should be suitable conceptually for product catalogs, procurement systems and industrial commerce platforms.

Before writing any code, do a detailed technical analysis and proposal. First interpret the problem, identify the real technical challenge, compare likely common student solutions, define the unique core innovation and explain why the proposed approach is different. Then research and recommend the most appropriate industrial-product dataset strategy, including the exact data sources, platforms, URLs, publishers, licenses/access terms and whether the data is real, curated or synthetic. Then propose the complete feature set, generic product architecture, end-to-end workflow, page/navigation structure, UI design, AI/ML approach, evidence and confidence architecture, validation methodology, replacement-matching algorithm, accuracy evaluation, scalability strategy, performance strategy, database/schema design, API architecture, technology comparison and final recommended technology stack. Also explain how Groq should be used and where deterministic algorithms, ML models, embeddings or other methods are more appropriate than LLM calls. Define the project folder structure and a convincing hackathon demo scenario.

The final prototype should demonstrate one strong end-to-end scenario, such as starting with incomplete or scattered information about an industrial product, extracting information from an image/document, identifying the product, discovering relevant attributes dynamically, enriching missing information, normalizing values, retrieving and comparing evidence from multiple sources, detecting conflicting values, validating technical consistency, matching the product to industry standards, calculating evidence-based confidence, showing Before & After changes, obtaining human approval, finding a technically compatible replacement if necessary, and finally generating a commerce-ready Product Intelligence Passport. The demonstrated product category is only an example; the underlying architecture must remain generic enough to support other industrial product categories.

Do not immediately start coding. First produce the complete technical proposal and wait for approval. Optimize the final solution for a student hackathon: it should be a working, reliable and impressive prototype with a genuinely strong technical approach, measurable accuracy, efficient processing, scalable architecture and explainable AI rather than a large collection of unfinished features. The central positioning should be: “An AI system that does not merely extract industrial product data, but builds a trustworthy product record from scattered evidence by enriching, validating, standardizing, explaining and verifying the information before making it commerce-ready.”
